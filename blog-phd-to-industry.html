Seven Ways to Be New: What Transferred From My PhD, and What Didn't

WhatsApp Facebook LinkedIn Instagram X Email

My PhD advisor had one rule: at least one of seven things had to be genuinely new. Four years in industry taught me which parts of that training actually mattered, and which did not.

My PhD advisor had one rule. To publish, at least one of these had to be genuinely new:

  • the way the input is represented
  • the model itself
  • performance beating everything in the literature
  • data unlike anything that already existed
  • lower cost than the alternatives
  • faster than the alternatives
  • an application nobody had built

Seven ways to be new. You needed one. I ended up with two: input representation, and data.

My thesis was Indian Sign Language recognition. The models I used were the ordinary ones everybody used. What was mine was what went into them, and what they ran on.

The ISL datasets in the literature used clean uniform backgrounds and put limits on what a signer could wear, to make recognition easier. I built one that didn't: filmed against a cluttered wall, with no restrictions on clothing or accessories, because the clean versions didn't survive contact with real signing.

I submitted in October 2021. A few months later I joined athenahealth as a data scientist and began finding out which parts of that training actually mattered here.

Four years there, and now working independently, I can sort it fairly cleanly.

What I expected to be different, and wasn't

When athenahealth hired me, HR told me plainly that this was not a research job. Product team, ML coming into the picture, strong people, but not research. He asked if I was sure. I said yes, and I arrived expecting no papers and no discussions.

That turned out to be wrong.

The data science team ran paper discussions. Informal ones. Somebody would find a paper worth reading, take it, and present it to everyone else. I gave one of them. During experimentation people read papers and quoted them at each other as a matter of course.

Every project had a leaderboard. People tried models, results went up, and we argued about what was worth comparing next.

So most of what I assumed I was importing was already in the room. Reading papers, running comparisons, coming prepared. None of that was mine to bring, and I'd have been wrong to think otherwise.

What actually differed

Writing things down when nothing forced me to

Confluence was already in use when I joined, and I read a lot of pages other people had written. Documentation was not missing at athenahealth.

But it followed the work. When you are running many experiments against a leaderboard in a short window, you have to write things down, because you will forget what you tried, what the data was, how many rows it had, what the numbers came out as. Other people need it too. Someone running a different experiment comes and asks, or finds that part of yours overlaps with theirs. That documentation existed because the work demanded it.

The gap was everywhere else. A support ticket. An ongoing code review. One standalone experiment that wasn't part of a series. Designing an inference. Isolated tasks with no leaderboard behind them. Most people didn't write much for those, and nothing was lost that anyone noticed at the time.

I wrote them up anyway.

One example. I ran stress testing on LLM models, which was fairly new on our side then and something I had not done before. A standard process already existed on a Confluence page. What I added was how I had actually understood and run it: which files to use, step one, step two, what each part means. Several people on the team used that afterwards.

So the difference was never that I documented and others didn't. On the work where documentation was obviously required, everybody wrote, and wrote well. I also wrote on the work where nobody would have missed it.

How deep you go

In my PhD the main work was getting the final numbers out of the sign language system. While training I used Adam, which at the time was considered one of the best optimisers available. I could have used it and left it there.

I went further, and compared it against the others, including the parameters inside each one.

The motivation was not one thing. Curiosity about what the others would do in my setting, and why. The possibility of a paper. And the knowledge that when I published the main work, a reviewer could ask whether I had tried other optimisers and why not. If I was going to have to answer that eventually, I preferred to answer it then. There was time, so the question off to the side was worth taking.

Industry asks the same question and stops sooner. When the first choice is not giving the results we need, we try others. We would still run a comparison. It would not go as deep as the one I ran in my PhD, because it does not need to.

The bar in a PhD is novelty, and the evidence for it. In industry the bar is the stakeholder requirement and the timeline.

What didn't

The novelty rule

The seven ways to be new are the right frame for a thesis. Industry work runs on a different one.

In a PhD, novelty is what decides. Without it there is no thesis and no paper.

In industry novelty is present, and often it is the thing a team is proudest of. What changes is the weight it carries in a decision. Work goes ahead because it meets a stakeholder requirement inside a timeline, and something genuinely new is welcome when it serves that. It is rarely the thing that has to be proved before anything can ship.

That is my experience rather than a claim about the field. There are companies whose growth comes directly from doing new things, and novel work happens inside ordinary product teams as well.

I still ask what's new here, because it's a useful question. In research, if the answer was "nothing", I had no thesis and no paper.

In industry the question carries less weight. Nobody was casual about what shipped and the standards were serious, but the bar was the stakeholder requirement rather than novelty. If what we had built met the requirement, the work was finished, whether or not anything about it was new.

The pace

My literature survey ran about a year before I started serious experimentation. Read, analyse, read more, and only then decide which experiment is worth running.

That is normal in research and unworkable in industry. I had to learn to act before I fully understood something, and to let the result close the gap. This was the hardest adjustment and the one I'm most grateful for.

The one that changed shape

Mastery

A PhD trains you to master one narrow area and become the person who knows it best. It's good advice and I believed it completely.

In my first goal-setting conversation at athenahealth, my manager asked what I wanted. I said I wanted to become an expert in one thing. He told me that wouldn't work there.

He was right, and it took me a while to see how. Depth still matters. You need one thing you genuinely own. But you also have to handle everything inside that thing's ecosystem well, and stay ready to master whatever comes next. The specialty is the anchor. It isn't the whole job.

What the PhD never gave me

Everything in this list I learned cold, on the job:

  • Deadlines set by someone else, against work I hadn't chosen
  • Prioritising my work against other people's
  • Working inside a codebase that other people own
  • Code review as a daily norm rather than an occasional favour
  • Cost and latency as things that decide the design, rather than numbers you report afterwards
  • Owning something after it ships
  • Explaining technical work to people who don't want the method, only what it means for them
  • Estimating how long something will take

Code review is the one I'd single out. Nothing in a PhD prepares you to have your work read line by line by people who will be maintaining it. It's also the fastest I have ever improved at writing code.

Estimation is the one I'm still working on. A PhD gives you almost no practice at saying how long something will take, because the honest answer is usually "until it works." I'm better at it now than I was. Not reliably right, but close enough to plan around, and still improving.

What I'd tell someone making the same move

Don't assume you're arriving with something rare. I did, and I was wrong. A good data science team already reads papers, already compares models, already prepares. You are not bringing different habits, only more consistent ones, applied to the small isolated tasks where nothing forces the issue.

Expect two things to work differently: how new your work is required to be, and how long you get to spend understanding something before you act. Neither research habit is wrong. Both were built for a different set of rules.

And take your manager seriously when they tell you the shape of expertise is different here. Mine was right.
