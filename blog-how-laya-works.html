Laya, Explained: The AI That Answers by Ticking Boxes

WhatsApp Facebook LinkedIn Instagram X Email

Most AI models answer by writing, while Laya answers by filling in an answer sheet. This post walks through how it does that, one picture per step, using the real numbers it produced on two shell commands. It then shows how Laya was trained to state honest probabilities, with a demo you can step through, and ends with where Laya came from and how it compares with Jev, the closed model whose interface it copies.

Part of this series

  • Part 1 (this post): Laya, explained. How the model reads a question and answers it.
  • Part 2: Does Laya work on real commands? Laya as a safety check on my AI coding agent's commands: few errors on a short terminal command set of 50 standard one-line commands, but it would have wrongly stopped about a third of my real, harmless ones.
  • Part 3: Teaching Laya my real commands. What changed after training it on 800 of my own commands labelled by Claude.

Before we start

What Laya is, in one paragraph

Laya is a small AI model released in September 2026 by a company called Convai Innovations. It is open: anyone can download it and run it on their own computer, free of charge, under a licence (Apache 2.0) that also allows changing it and using it in products. Its job is narrow: you give it a situation and a question with a fixed set of possible answers, and it tells you how likely each answer is, without ever writing a sentence.

Its makers call it a "System 1" model. The phrase comes from the psychologist Daniel Kahneman, whose book Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) describes two ways the mind works. System 1 is fast and automatic: you see a face and know it is angry without working it out. System 2 is slow and deliberate: you multiply 17 by 24 step by step. Chat models, which reason in words, are the slow kind. Laya is meant to be the fast kind: an instant judgement call, in a few hundredths of a second.

How Laya differs from a normal chat model

A chat model such as ChatGPT or Claude answers by writing. It produces its reply one small piece of text at a time, choosing each piece from everything it knows, and it can write anything: a sentence, a list, a story, a wrong fact. If you ask it "is this command dangerous?", you get a sentence such as "Yes, this command deletes a folder", and your program then has to read that sentence and work out what it meant.

Laya answers by choosing. You hand it the question and the allowed answers, for example "true" and "false", and it hands back a number for each: 70 out of 100 for "false", 30 for "true". It cannot write a sentence, so it cannot wander off the question or invent an answer that was not on the list. It also does its work in a single pass through the model, which is why it takes a few hundredths of a second where a chat model takes seconds.

Think of an exam: the chat model is a student writing an essay answer one word after another, while Laya is a student with a multiple-choice sheet, deciding how much to trust each printed option. Everything below follows that picture.

The example used throughout

An AI coding agent is a program that writes and runs code for you, and mine (Claude Code) runs terminal commands on my laptop. Some commands delete things. Throughout this post the situation is one such command and the question is whether it is destructive. Every number in the figures is Laya's real output, produced on my MacBook.

Laya comes in three saved versions. A saved version of a trained model is called a checkpoint, and the three differ in what extra training they received. I use the one named typed-decisions, which its makers trained further on four kinds of business decision: customer-service messages, invoices, security incidents and logs of what AI agents did. The last two are the closest to my question. The other two are the base English version and a multilingual version.

At a glance

  • The answer sheet. Laya turns your question into one block of plain text, laid out like an exam answer sheet: the question, each allowed answer with an empty box in front of it, then the situation.
  • One read. A reading network with 28 layers reads the whole sheet in a single pass. By the end, each empty box holds a summary of how well its answer fits.
  • From scores to probabilities. A small scorer gives each box a number, and the gap between the numbers is turned into a probability for each answer, after a correction called the temperature that keeps the probabilities honest.
  • Paid for honesty. Laya was trained with a scoring rule that punishes confident wrong answers hard, so its best strategy is to state exactly the probability it believes.
  • Laya and Jev. They share the same idea and the same interface, but Laya is open and runs on your machine while Jev is a closed paid service.

Step 1

The question becomes an answer sheet

The figure below shows exactly what Laya receives for my example. Rather than a chat message, it is one block of plain text, always written in the same order: first the kind of question (yes/no) and the question itself, then the allowed answers, each printed with an empty box in front of it, and last the situation to judge, which here is the command. For a yes/no question the two answers are always "false" and "true", each with a short description.

The empty box is not just a drawing. In the actual text that Laya reads, each box is written as the word [MASK]. Here is the full text Laya received for my example, exactly as it was sent:

[CLS] noul question: Is this shell command destructive or irreversible? … [SEP]
[MASK] false: safe or easily undone
[MASK] true: destructive or irreversible [SEP]
{"tool": "Bash", "command": "rm -rf ~/projects/old-site", "cwd": "/Users/…"} [SEP]

[CLS] marks the start, [SEP] is a divider between sections, and noul is Laya's name for a yes/no question. Why [MASK]? The reading network inside Laya first learned language through a fill-in-the-blank game: one word in a sentence was replaced by [MASK] and the network had to guess the missing word. So the network is used to treating [MASK] as "a blank to fill in from the surrounding text". Laya puts one blank in front of each answer and later reads what the network filled in.

Before the text is read, it is cut into pieces called tokens. A token is a word or a part of a word: "destructive" is one token, while "overwriting" becomes two, "over" and "writing". My example text is 97 tokens long. This checkpoint can read up to 1,024 tokens; anything beyond that is dropped.

Step 2

One read, twenty-eight rounds

The reading network is an existing open model called ModernBERT-large, published in December 2024, which Laya's makers built on rather than starting from scratch. It has 28 layers. Picture each layer as one round of re-reading the whole sheet. In every round, each token looks at other tokens and adjusts what it "means" in this context. In most rounds a token looks at the 128 tokens around it; in every third round it looks at the whole sheet.

The tokens it is reading are the ones in the text above, nothing else. Here are all 97 of them for my example, written the way a Python program would list them; a leading space inside a token means it began a new word:

tokens = [
    # the start marker, the kind of question, and the question
    "[CLS]", "n", "oul", " question", ":", " Is", " this", " shell", " command",
    " destructive", " or", " irreversible", "?", " De", "let", "ing", " files", ",",
    " over", "writing", " history", ",", " dropping", " data", ",", " force", "-", "p",
    "ushing", ",", " killing", " processes", ",", " or", " sending", " private", " data",
    " off", " the", " machine", " all", " count", ".", "[SEP]",
    # the two answers, each with its empty box
    "[MASK]", " false", ":", " safe", " or", " easily", " und", "one",
    "[MASK]", " true", ":", " destructive", " or", " irreversible", "[SEP]",
    # the situation: the command, and the folder it would run in
    '{"', "tool", '":', ' "', "B", "ash", '",', ' "', "command", '":', ' "',
    "rm", " -", "rf", " ~", "/", "projects", "/", "old", "-", "site", '",',
    ' "', "c", "wd", '":', ' "/', "Users", "/", "…", "/", "cl", "a", "ude", '"}', "[SEP]",
]

So the "context" a token adjusts to is made of the question's words, the two printed answers and the command's pieces. The token rm means one thing when followed by - rf and a folder name (delete a whole folder, no questions asked) and something milder when followed by - f and one file name. The word "destructive" in the question, the word "Deleting" in its description and the piece rm in the command sit far apart on the sheet, and the rounds are what connect them.

The important part for us is what happens to the two blanks. A blank has no meaning of its own, so in each round it takes its meaning entirely from what it sees: the question, the printed answer next to it, and the command. By round 28 the blank in front of "true" holds, in effect, a summary of "how well does true: destructive fit this command?". Figure 2 shows this reading as arcs between tokens, with the two blanks in orange.

All 28 rounds happen in one pass through the model. The bottom half of Figure 2 shows the contrast with a chat model: it writes its answer one token at a time and runs the whole model again for every new token, so a 50-word answer costs about 50 passes. That difference is where Laya's speed comes from.

Step 3

Each box gets a score

After the 28 rounds, each blank holds a list of 1,024 numbers, which is how the network stores "meaning". On top of the reading network, Laya's makers added two more layers of the same kind plus a small piece of arithmetic they call the scorer, all trained from scratch. The scorer looks at each blank's 1,024 numbers and boils them down to a single number, the box's score, where a higher score means "this answer fits better". A score means nothing on its own, because only the gap between the two boxes matters, and Figure 3 shows those scores for two commands.

For git status, the "false" box beat the "true" box by 1.73. For rm -rf ~/projects/old-site, the two boxes scored 0.19 and 0.16, almost a tie. In other words, the model as its makers shipped it barely separates a harmless command from one that deletes a folder. That is the weakness Parts 2 and 3 measure.

Step 4

Scores become probabilities

A score on its own is just a number; what you want is a probability, a number between 0% and 100% that says how likely each answer is. Laya gets there with a fixed rule that depends only on the gap between the two scores. If the two boxes score the same, each answer gets 50%. The further one box pulls ahead, the more of the 100% it takes: a lead of 1 point gives it about 73%, a lead of 2 points about 88%, and a lead of 3 points about 95%. (The rule is called the softmax; the name does not matter.) The probability of "true" is Laya's answer to "how likely is this command to be destructive?"

Before that rule is applied, Laya divides every score by a fixed number called the temperature. Dividing both scores by the same number shrinks the gap between them, and a smaller gap means a less extreme probability, closer to 50%. The temperature never changes which answer wins; it only changes how confident the model sounds. A temperature of 1 changes nothing, a temperature above 1 makes the model more modest, and one below 1 makes it more confident. For yes/no questions in this checkpoint the temperature is 1.98, so every gap is roughly halved.

Why is it there? Models trained the way Laya was tend to come out over-confident: they say "90%" on answers that turn out right only 70% of the time. The temperature is the standard correction. Laya's makers fitted it after training, on a set of questions with known answers: they tried many values and kept the one under which the stated probabilities matched reality best, meaning that of all the answers given "70%", about 70 in 100 were in fact right. They fitted a separate value for each kind of question (yes/no, pick-one with few options, pick-one with many options, rate-on-a-scale), and on their own test questions the fitted temperatures cut the model's calibration error, the average gap between stated and actual accuracy, from 0.47 to 0.08.

Figure 4 shows the result for both commands, and the arithmetic behind it is short. For git status the "false" box led by 1.73 points. Fed straight into the rule, that lead is worth 84.9% for "false" and 15.1% for "true"; those are the "before temperature" numbers in the figure. Dividing by 1.98 shrinks the lead to 0.87 points, which the rule turns into 70.5% and 29.5%. So Laya's final answer is a 29.5% chance that git status is destructive. For rm -rf ~/projects/old-site the lead was only 0.03 points, which gives 50.7% against 49.3% before the temperature and 50.3% against 49.7% after it: an even split either way, because there was almost no gap to shrink.

Two things follow. The temperature can make the model more modest, but it cannot create a gap the scorer did not find, which is why the rm -rf answer stays at an even split. And Part 3 of this series shows what happens when the temperature is left unfitted after new training: the probabilities stop meaning what they say, even when the ranking of commands is good.

Why it cannot invent answers

It can be wrong, but it cannot make things up

Laya only divides probability between the answers printed on the sheet. It has no way to write an answer that is not on the sheet. That removes one whole class of failure that chat models have, inventing a plausible-sounding answer, which people call hallucination. It can still be wrong, by giving the wrong answer the higher probability. My example shows exactly that kind of failure: a 50% chance of "false" for a command that deletes a folder.

How it learned

A forecaster paid for honesty

A model's numbers come from training: showing it many examples and nudging its internal numbers (Laya has 421 million of them, called parameters) so that its answers improve. Laya's makers used reinforcement learning, which means learning by trial and reward: the model answers a practice question, gets a reward that depends on how good the answer was, and adjusts to earn more reward next time.

Laya's makers call their method RLCD, short for Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions; TypeSafe AI uses the same name for how it trained Jev, the rival model described at the end of this post. Calibrated means that the stated confidence matches reality: of all the times Laya says "70% likely", the answer should be right about 70 times in 100. One practice round has four parts:

  • Try. Laya answers a practice question whose right answer is already known. During practice it adds a little random noise to its scores, so it answers the same question several times, sharing the probability slightly differently each time.
  • Score. Each try is marked with a proper scoring rule, explained below. The less probability a try gave the right answer, the more points it loses.
  • Compare. The tries are compared with their own average. A try that lost fewer points than the average is encouraged, and one that lost more is discouraged. Learning from "better than my usual", rather than from the raw score, keeps training steady; it is the idea behind GRPO, a method also used to train reasoning models.
  • Adjust. Laya's parameters move a little toward the tries that did better, and the next practice question begins. The round repeats over thousands of questions.

A proper scoring rule marks a forecast so that bluffing never pays. The easiest one to picture is the log score, which Figure 5 shows in four cases: say "90% true" and be right and you lose only 0.11 points, but say "90% true" and be wrong and you lose 2.30 points, twenty times more, while a modest "60% true" loses 0.51 or 0.92 either way.

Under a rule like this, the best long-run strategy is to state exactly the probability you believe. On a single question with a clear answer, giving more probability to the right answer always scores better, so honesty comes from the many practice questions that look alike but turn out differently. Suppose ten customer messages all read "Can I switch to the yearly plan?" and seven of them turn out to be billing questions. If Laya says 70% billing for each one, it loses 0.61 points per message on average. Saying 99% loses 1.39 on average, because each of the three non-billing messages costs 4.61. Saying 50% loses 0.69. No other probability loses fewer points than 70%, which is exactly how often the answer is billing.

A weather forecaster paid this way would say "70% rain" only on days when rain really is that likely, because bluffing loses points over a season. That is why Laya's probabilities are meant to be honest. Its code combines the log score with a second rule of the same family (the spherical score) and adds a third for scale questions.

Figure 6 lets you step through one practice round. It uses a customer-service message rather than my shell command, because messages like it are among the questions Laya practised on. The four tries and the size of the final adjustment are made up for illustration; the points lost are calculated with the real log score.

The training makes Laya's probabilities honest on questions like its practice questions, and nothing guarantees the same on a different kind of question. Laya's makers report that, on their own benchmarks, the gap between how sure it says it is and how often it is right averages 8 points, after re-fitting the temperature for each kind of question. On the 50 test commands of my short terminal command set in Part 2, Laya as shipped had an average gap of 34 points.

What you can ask

Three kinds of questions

Every question has one of three types, and you write the options yourself, in plain words, when you ask. A yes/no question returns the probability of "true". A pick-one question returns a probability for each option in a list you supply. A rate-on-a-scale question returns a probability for each level you define, plus the average level. Because the options are written into the sheet, a new question needs no retraining. Figure 7 shows Laya's real answers to all three kinds for my rm -rf example, asked in one call: it recognised that the command deletes data, and was unsure how far the damage could reach.

Speed and limits

Fast, and honest about its limits

Its model card (the makers' own description, published with the model) reports 39.5 milliseconds per question for the English checkpoint on an NVIDIA T4, a common cloud graphics card. A millisecond is a thousandth of a second, so that is about 25 questions per second. On my MacBook, three questions about one command took 94 milliseconds. For comparison, asking Claude Opus 5 the same yes/no question through its API took 2.3 seconds in Part 2.

The model card is also frank about limits: on decision tasks it was not trained for, the base version scores 0.362 where random guessing would score 0.318, and with more than about 20 options it gets weak. Its makers therefore present it as a fast base to fine-tune on your own data, fine-tuning being further training on your own examples, and my two experiments test exactly that. Part 2 found that, used as shipped, its danger score rose with the length of a command, so it would have stopped me on 37% of my real commands, almost all of them harmless. Part 3 trains it on my real commands and measures what changes.

Laya and Jev

Is Laya the same as Jev?

Jev is a decision model from a company called TypeSafe AI, announced on 15 September 2026 and named after the economist William Stanley Jevons. It takes the same kind of input and returns the same kind of output as Laya: a situation and typed questions in, probabilities out, no text. TypeSafe calls its models "System One" models, and Laya's author describes Laya as the same idea. Laya's server even accepts requests in Jev's format, so code written for one can call the other.

Laya's author is Nandakishor M, founder and CEO of Convai Innovations, a company in Kasaragod, Kerala, India. He traces Laya back to two papers he published before Jev existed. The first, in March 2025, described SalesRLAgent, a model trained by reinforcement learning to predict how likely a sales conversation is to end in a sale. The second, in September 2025, was about judging how reliable a language model's answer is likely to be before the model writes it.

Laya itself appeared on 18 September 2026, three days after Jev, when its code and model files were published. In a post the same day, written in reply to Jev's launch, he said he had decided to "take everything I learned from my March 2025 and September 2025 papers" and build a completely open decision model. So the idea grew out of his own earlier work, while the release date and the Jev-compatible request format followed Jev's launch. The deal around each model is very different, and the table shows how.

A few table words explained. "Weights" are the model's parameters, so "open weights" means you can download the trained model itself. An "API" is a paid web service you send requests to. "Transformer" is the family of neural network that both chat models and Laya's reader belong to. The two speed figures are not a fair race: Laya's is measured on a local graphics card, and Jev's includes the trip over the internet. The Banking77 row comes from Laya's own model card; it measures how well each model picks the right one of 77 customer intents.

In short: pick Jev if you want a hosted service that handles long inputs and many options; pick Laya if you want to see inside the model, run it on your own machine, and train it on your own data.

What to remember

  • A chat model answers by writing; Laya answers by giving a probability to each answer you wrote down. So it cannot invent an answer, though it can back the wrong one.
  • It reads the whole question and situation in one pass, which is why it answers in tens of milliseconds where a chat model takes seconds.
  • It was trained to state honest probabilities, but as shipped it barely told a folder deletion from a harmless status check; its makers intend it to be trained further on your own data.
  • Jev offers the same interface as a closed, paid service; Laya is the open one you can inspect and train.
